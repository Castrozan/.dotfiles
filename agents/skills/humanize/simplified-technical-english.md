<why_this_exists>
Agents drift into private vocabulary: they coin a term for a thing that already has a name, then reuse it as though the
reader had agreed to it, and the text reads fluent while carrying less meaning every paragraph. ASD-STE100, the
controlled English the aerospace industry wrote so a technician cannot misread a maintenance instruction, is the
correction, and its discipline transfers to anything a human or another agent must parse once and then act on. Apply it
to reports, instructions, commits, tickets, and documentation; leave it off opinion and persuasion, where flat and
literal is the wrong register.
</why_this_exists>

<one_meaning_per_word>
Pick one word for one thing and reuse it every time, because rotating synonyms for the same referent forces the reader
to decide whether you meant something new. Hold each word to one part of speech, so oil is a noun and you apply oil to
the valve rather than oiling it. Prefer the plainest common word over the impressive one.
</one_meaning_per_word>

<no_invented_vocabulary>
Never coin a project term in passing. When a thing needs a name that is neither common English nor already used by the
code, define it once at first use in the same sentence and then use it unchanged; if you cannot define it in a clause,
you do not need the term, you need the plain description. Reuse the name the code already carries rather than a
prettier one you prefer.
</no_invented_vocabulary>

<sentence_construction>
Active voice with the actor named, unless the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant. Simple tenses, so write we
received the report rather than we have received the report. One instruction per sentence. Around 20 words for an
instruction and 25 for a description; a sentence past that is carrying two ideas, so split it. Stack at most three
words in a noun phrase, so a fuel pump valve reads and a high pressure fuel pump inlet valve assembly does not. Keep
the subject, the verb, and the articles explicit instead of dropping words to save space, because the dropped word is
what makes the sentence ambiguous.
</sentence_construction>

<never_simplify_by_deletion>
The failure mode of this discipline is silent information loss: a rewrite reads cleaner because a condition, a number,
a scope qualifier, or a caveat quietly vanished. Every rewrite carries every fact the original carried. Where plain
phrasing would cost precision, keep the longer phrasing and say why, rather than shipping the smoother sentence.
</never_simplify_by_deletion>

<not_a_costume>
This is a discipline, not a register to perform. Short sentences do not make an analysis childish, and they never
license dropping the reasoning a reader needs; an argument translated into simple sentences must still argue the same
thing. Judgment stays, decoration goes.
</not_a_costume>

<provenance>
Distilled from ASD-STE100 Issue 9, the AeroSpace and Defence Industries Association of Europe specification, which
carries 53 writing rules plus a dictionary of approved words. This chapter takes the rules and the principle under
them, not the word list, which stays ASD's own document at https://www.asd-ste100.org. Re-pull on a new issue of the
standard rather than drifting with it.
</provenance>
